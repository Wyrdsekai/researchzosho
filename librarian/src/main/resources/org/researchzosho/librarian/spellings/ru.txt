# Tchaïkovski, the scholarly Čajkovskij, Polish Czajkowski. The soft and hard signs (ь, ъ) are left out, as passports and most records
# leave them out. These rules say how each sound is written in each tradition, so all of those spellings compare as one name.

# comparison. The traditions share letters for different sounds, so some sounds are one here:
#  - ч and х: English writes ч as ch, German and the scholars write х as ch (Chruschtschow). kh, ch, tch, tsch, cz, č and a lone h are one.
#  - ш and ж: German writes both as sch (Schukow for Жуков). sh, sch, sz, zh, š, ž are one, and French j before ou (Joukov).
#  - й, ы and и: y, j and i are one (Chaykovskiy, Čajkovskij, Chaikovskii), and so are ya, ja, ia and yu, ju, iu, iou.
#  - е after a vowel and at the start: ye, je, ie and e are one (Yevgeniy, Jewgeni, Evgeni); ё as yo, jo, io, ë or e is e (Pyotr, Petr).
#  - the endings: -ev and -ov are one (-ёв is written both ways: Gorbachev, Gorbatschow), -off and -ow are -ov, and -ina, French -ine.
# French ш, written ch (Pouchkine), meets the English ч instead; French ж before any vowel but ou reads as the German glide j. The male and
# female forms (Ivanov, Ivanova) stay two names.
