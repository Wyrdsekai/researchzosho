# A Korean name in Hangul is written in Latin letters by the Revised Romanization of 2000, the first way here: 김민준 is Gim Minjun. Records
# before 2000 and outside South Korea write McCune-Reischauer (Kim Minjun, Pak Chŏng-hŭi, Ch'oe), often without its breves and apostrophes,
# and a family keeps the spelling it chose long ago: Kim, Lee, Park, Choi, Jung, Yoon, Shin, Oh, Ahn, Rhee. These rules say how each sound is
# written in those ways, so Gim and Kim, Bak, Pak and Park, I, Yi, Lee and Rhee compare as one name. A Hangul syllable is taken apart into
# its letters (jamo), which have one code point for a consonant that begins a syllable and another for one that ends it.

# comparison. The ways share letters for different sounds, so some sounds are one here:
#  - g and k, d and t, b and p, j and ch are one (Gim, Kim; Bak, Pak), and doubled kk, tt, pp, ss, jj or tch is the single letter.
#  - ㅓ is eo, ŏ, or u in a family's own spelling (Jeon, Chŏn, Chun; Seong, Sung; Heo, Hur): eo is u. ㅡ is eu (Eun, Seung) and stays eu.
#  - oo and ou are u (Yoon, Moon, Koo), ee is i (Lee, Hee), and hui, hŭi and hee are one (Chung-hee).
#  - an h or r before no vowel is only a spelling (Oh, Ahn, Sohn, Park, Hur).
#  - at the start of a word l and rh are r; ri and yi are i and ry is y (Lee, Rhee, Yi and I; Lim and Im; Ryu and Yu); r is n before any
#    other vowel (Roh and No, Ra and Na), as the two Koreas write those names one way each.
# 여 (Yeo) and 유 (Yu), 연 (Yeon) and 윤 (Yun) are one here, as records write them all Yu and Yun. MR without its breves writes ㅓ as o (Chong),
# which this reads as ㅗ.
